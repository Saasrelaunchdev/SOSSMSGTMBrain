# Running gtm-builder on SOS SMS, Run 2: Demand (Customers)

The skill is `B2B SaaS Go-To-Market Builder`. This run sells the customer side of a two-sided marketplace for emergency home repair: a person with a problem at home texts one number and gets a vetted contractor with an arrival time. The buyer is a consumer, deciding alone, in minutes, under stress. That breaks the skill's ICP, buying committee, channels, and pricing, and changes what loyalty means.

Read this before Phase 1. Without it the run produces a three-person buying committee for someone standing in water at 2 a.m., subscription tiers for a one-off emergency call, and a LinkedIn campaign aimed at homeowners.

## Phase 1. Product Feature Matrix

**Assumes:** one product with one buyer, its features scored on demand and competitiveness.

**Why it breaks:** SOS SMS has two buyers and two feature lists. Rating them together blends two demand curves into one quadrant chart that describes neither side.

**Instead:** rate only the items in `GTM-BRIEF.md` Section 3: nine from the source and three candidates. Run Steps 1.3 to 1.5 as written. Seed the scores with Bryan's ratings, labeled as hypotheses, and let the Step 1.1 research move them. Rate the relaunch guarantee like any other item. Its handling is decided (resolution register R17): SOS SMS pays the second contractor's trip charge, and it stays out of copy until dispatch supports it. Steps 1.6 and 1.7 run unchanged.

**Result to watch for.** Bryan rated "Text-only access" and "Private by design" as Unique To Us, and the 90-minute guarantee as something most vendors can do. If Step 1.1 finds the reverse, text access common and refund-backed arrival windows rare, the positioning moves from "no app" to the guarantee. The guarantee's scope (decision 3) then becomes the most important sentence in the run.

## Phase 2. Competitive Market Analysis

**Assumes:** the competitors are reviewed on G2, Capterra, and Trustpilot. Step 2.1 crawls them and Step 2.7 compares four and five star reviews against one to three star reviews.

**Why it breaks:** customers do not review emergency repair on G2. They review the contractor who came, or did not come, on maps listings, and they complain about platforms on consumer sites and forums.

**Instead:** keep Step 2.7's question, what satisfied customers say they got and what unsatisfied ones say they did not, and ask it of these sources:

- Maps and search reviews of the emergency contractors that rank in the launch metro. One-star reviews of emergency calls describe exactly what customers fear
- Customer-side app store reviews of slot 1 and slot 4 competitors
- Trustpilot and BBB complaints from customers of those competitors
- Reddit homeowner and home repair communities, and local community groups

Slot 5, doing it yourself or waiting, is not a company. Profile it from the most viewed shutoff and temporary-fix guides and the forum threads around them. Profile slot 6 from warranty and insurer claim process pages and from complaints about how long their contractors take.

**Our own row.** Fill it from `GTM-BRIEF.md` Section 1, not a crawl, and state strengths and weaknesses by design, with the weaknesses written plainly: no track record yet, and open only in zones at density. There is nothing live to review.

## Phase 4. ICP

**Assumes:** "ICP Level 1, MVP to PMF" and "ICP Level 2, Growth", with firmographic rows: revenue, team size, business model.

**Why it breaks:** the buyer is a household, not a company, and the thing that defines them is a trigger event, not a stage.

**Instead,** the B2C rewrite:

- **Level 1.** Has no go-to contractor for this trade, because they are new to the home or the area or have never needed one.
- **Level 2.** Has a go-to contractor who does not answer at night or on weekends.

Replace the firmographic rows. "Revenue, $" becomes household income band, but only if the research ties it to willingness to pay a trip charge, otherwise drop it. "Team Size" becomes owner-occupier, renter, or small landlord. "Business Model" becomes how they have paid for repairs before: directly, through a warranty, or through insurance. Add rows for the trigger (what broke, at what hour), home age and type, whether they have a card and a smartphone to hand, and how they found help last time.

**Result to watch for.** Under the product-led plan both levels are reached mainly in the moment, through search ads and problem-solving pages, which is the most expensive place to be found. The product loops (saved number, post-job share) are what make the second request from a household cheaper than the first.

## Phase 5. Buying Committee

**Collapses.** Whoever is at home with the problem decides, at that moment. Fill one column honestly, note the collapse, and do not invent a Manager and a Decision Maker.

The one exception is a renter. The landlord is a second decision maker and often the payer, which is why the demand side was decided as homeowners (brief decision 1) rather than assumed.

**Step 5.2** is the founding customer area rather than a design partner in the B2B sense: the neighborhoods inside Run 1's founding contractor coverage. It is a geographic boundary that keeps the 90-minute guarantee honest. Every Phase 8 asset targets inside it and nowhere else.

---

## Phase 6, the fabrication risk

Phase 6 asks for a "Supporting Proof Point" and a "Headline (Social Proof)" in each of nine copy drills. Eighteen fields asking for evidence.

SOS SMS has none. No customers, no jobs, no arrival times on record. In every drill the social proof headline is a method line and the proof point names the record that will show the claim is true. Method and process are legitimate substitutes for the social proof headline: how it works in three texts, what the trip charge buys, and what happens when the 90-minute window is missed. That signals seriousness without claiming a result.

**Two banned forms this side reaches for:** an average arrival time, and a count of homes helped. Neither exists.

**The safety line.** Any drill that invites an urgent text carries the 911 line from `GTM-BRIEF.md` Section 5. It is not optional copy.

**Cross-side drift.** Check every headline against `GTM-BRIEF.md` Section 6 before closing the phase.

This is where a fabricated testimonial enters a run that was otherwise honest, because the template asks nine times and templates get filled.

## Phase 7. Customer Journey Funnel

**Runs as written,** with one exception. Stage 7, "Renewal and Loyalty", assumes a recurring contract. A household has an emergency rarely, so loyalty here means the number is saved in their phone and recommended to a neighbor. Measure those, not repeat purchase.

**Add these to Primary Journey Blockers.** Each is an infrastructure problem that will look like a funnel problem:

- **Can the buyer pay.** The trip charge is paid at night, by a link in a text. No card to hand, or a card that declines, ends the job before dispatch.
- **Does the link look like a scam.** A payment link by text from a number the customer has never seen matches a common fraud pattern. Trust has to be earned before the link arrives: who is texting, why, and what the charge is.
- **Can the text get through.** Business texting needs the sending number registered with carriers. In the United States that means 10DLC or toll-free verification. Unregistered traffic gets filtered, and the customer sees silence.
- **What if no one accepts.** What the customer is told, and when. This is the moment the 90-minute guarantee is tested, and decision 3 answers it: the customer is told within 15 minutes and is not charged.

## Phase 8. Assets and Collaterals

**Assumes three campaigns, hardcoded:** LinkedIn Outreach via HeyReach, Cold Email via Instantly, and LinkedIn Ads.

**Why it breaks:** nobody prospects a homeowner into an emergency, and SOS SMS has no sales team (brief, Classification). Demand arrives with the event, and the customer goes looking for a fix.

**Instead,** keep the three-channel by three-funnel-stage grid with three channels where customers are already trying to solve the problem, decided by the founders on 2026-10-01:

1. **Paid search, bottom of funnel.** Google Ads on emergency and problem keywords ("burst pipe", "water leaking through ceiling", "emergency plumber near me"), then Microsoft Ads. Geofenced to zones at density and scheduled to covered hours, with text-from-ad where Google's message asset rules allow it (S72). Google Local Services Ads are not open to SOS SMS: their policy forbids reselling leads and re-routing requests to businesses not listed in the ad (S70).
2. **Problem-solving pages.** Organic pages that answer the urgent question first (how to shut off the water, what to do about a sewer backup), with a text button and the safety line. They rank in search and get cited in AI answers.
3. **Product loops.** The product spreads itself: stop-the-water help in the first reply, the saved contact card, the post-job share, Text READY. Owned channels are what bring the cost per job down over time.

**The constraint on paid search is supply, not appetite.** An ad that works where too few contractors are available produces refunds under the 90-minute guarantee. Ads run only in zones and hours where Run 1's density target is met, and pause when acceptance drops.

**SMS as a marketing channel.** Marketing texts need separate opt-in consent (S31). Transactional texts stay transactional.

## Phase 9. Pricing and Packaging

**Assumes a subscription.** Value metrics per user, seat, usage, outcome, or capability, and four plans arranged as Decoy, Hero, and Anchor.

**Why it breaks:** a customer buys one emergency call, rarely. Four plans describe nothing they buy.

**Instead, build the customer side of the take rate:**

1. The trip charge: $99, set by SOS SMS per trade and zone, covering the visit and credited to the repair (decision 5).
2. The SOS SMS fee on top: $49 per dispatched request, always shown as its own line (decision 2).
3. The 90-minute refund as a cost line: planned at 5 percent of requests for misses SOS SMS causes, funded from the SOS SMS fee; contractor-caused misses are funded from the trip charge (decision 3, Section 6 row 1).
4. The subsidy question, shared with Run 1: which side carries SOS SMS's revenue, and why.

If tiers come up, the natural shape is a household membership with reduced or waived trip charges. It competes directly with home warranties, slot 6. Flag it as a later option and do not design it in this run.

**Step 9.3, ROI anchoring, still runs and is the strongest artifact here.** Anchor 2, the alternative comparison, is what the customer would otherwise pay for an after-hours call found by search: any after-hours premium plus any trip fee, from the Phase 2 research, dated and sourced. The cost of waiting until morning is the other natural anchor; the water damage claim average (S57, B) is the cited figure for it, and no other number is used.

## Lead Magnet Scorecard

Runs as written. A home emergency readiness scorecard fits well: do you know where your water shutoff is, do you have a contractor who answers at night. It ends with saving the number. Its outputs are scores and steps, never damage-cost figures without a source.

## 10-Slide Deck

Runs as written, once per run. A combined marketplace deck is a separate step after both runs. Its own prompt's rule on unknown metrics applies with extra force, because by then the caveats are two briefs back: no invented number, and every SOS SMS figure shown as decided, a planning value, or illustrative.
