# Running gtm-builder on SOS SMS, Run 1: Supply (Contractors)

The skill is `B2B SaaS Go-To-Market Builder`. SOS SMS is a two-sided marketplace for emergency home repair, and this run sells its contractor side: a free way for trade contractors to get urgent, matched jobs with the trip charge collected before they roll. A contractor is a small business, so the skill's B2B shape mostly holds. What breaks is the subscription pricing, the software review sources, the LinkedIn channel stack, and the assumption that there is only one buyer.

Read this before Phase 1. Without it the run produces four subscription tiers for a product that is free to join, a G2 analysis of a category that is not on G2, and a LinkedIn outreach campaign aimed at plumbers.

## Phase 1. Product Feature Matrix

**Assumes:** one product with one buyer, its features scored on demand and competitiveness.

**Why it breaks:** SOS SMS has two buyers and two feature lists. Rating them together lets a customer item like "Text-only access" carry a contractor's score, and blends two demand curves into one quadrant chart that describes neither side.

**Instead:** rate only the items in `GTM-BRIEF.md` Section 3: six from the source and three candidates. Run Steps 1.3 to 1.5 as written, because two axes fit platform features. Seed the scores with Bryan's ratings, labeled as hypotheses, and let the Step 1.1 research move them. Steps 1.6 and 1.7, the demand driver, run unchanged and feed the lead magnet and the Phase 8 top of funnel asset.

**Result to watch for.** Bryan rated "Zero cost to join" as Unique To Us. If Step 1.1 finds that contractors already have free ways to be found (slot 2), the item drops to "A few others", and the differentiator becomes the guaranteed trip charge, which a free listing does not collect. That would move Phase 3's positioning from price to getting paid for showing up.

## Phase 2. Competitive Market Analysis

**Assumes:** the competitors are reviewed on G2, Capterra, and Trustpilot. Step 2.1 crawls them and Step 2.7 compares four and five star reviews against one to three star reviews.

**Why it breaks:** a contractor's alternatives are lead marketplaces, their own listings, word of mouth, and answering services. G2 and Capterra carry software. A search there for emergency job sources is likely to return field service management software, which is a tool a contractor runs the business on, not another source of jobs. Do not let it into the slate.

**Instead:** keep Step 2.7's question, what satisfied contractors say they got and what unsatisfied ones say they did not, and ask it of these sources:

- Pro-side app store reviews of each slot 1 and slot 4 competitor
- Reddit trade communities and trade Facebook groups
- BBB and Trustpilot complaints filed by contractors rather than homeowners
- Contractor YouTube reviews of the platforms

Slots 3 and 5 are not companies. Profile word of mouth from the founding cohort interviews in Step 5.2. Profile the hire-it option from answering service price pages and from after-hours dispatcher job postings: pay band, hours, and time to hire are its cost and its weakness.

**Our own row.** Fill it from `GTM-BRIEF.md` Section 1, not a crawl, and mark strengths and weaknesses `{TBD}`. There is nothing live to review.

## Phase 4. ICP

**Assumes:** "ICP Level 1, MVP to PMF" and "ICP Level 2, Growth". Those are stages of a software company.

**Why it breaks:** a plumbing shop has no MVP stage. The levels need to describe how far the contractor has got with the problem SOS SMS solves, which is getting paid emergency work.

**Instead,** the maturity reading:

- **Level 1.** Takes emergency calls only when they arrive by word of mouth. No system for after-hours work and no paid job source.
- **Level 2.** Already pays for jobs, through per-lead platforms, search ads, or an answering service, and wants emergency volume without the per-lead cost.

Keep "Revenue, $" as the business's revenue band. Read "Team Size" as owner-operator, owner plus one to five technicians, or larger shop. Keep "Business Model" and read it as how they price work: flat rate, time and materials, and whether they charge an after-hours premium. Add rows for trades and licenses held, service radius, after-hours availability, and current job sources.

**Result to watch for.** Level 2 has the clearest Phase 9 anchor, what they pay per job today, and is the likely first ICP. Level 1 may not have the spare capacity to take a job at 2 a.m., which would make it a poor fit for the 90-minute guarantee however much it likes the price.

## Phase 5. Buying Committee

**Holds for shops with technicians.** User: the technician who rolls. Manager: the dispatcher or office manager, who decides which jobs get accepted. Decision Maker: the owner. Fill the Manager's "Potential Reasons to Block" row carefully. Likely blocks are an after-hours schedule that is already full, and job offers by text that go around the dispatch board.

**Collapses for owner-operators,** who are all three columns. Fill one column honestly and note the collapse. Do not invent three personas.

**Step 5.2 is the deliverable of this run.** For a pre-launch marketplace, the Design Partner profile is the founding contractor cohort, and it decides where SOS SMS launches. Define:

- The launch metro, trades, and coverage area (`GTM-BRIEF.md` open decision 1)
- The minimum number of active contractors per trade and area for the 90-minute guarantee to be honest. `{TBD}`. Phase 9 and Run 2 both need this density number
- What founding contractors get for joining early, and what they commit to

Run 2 cannot start Phase 4 without this.

## Phase 6, the fabrication risk

Phase 6 asks for a "Supporting Proof Point" and a "Headline (Social Proof)" in each of nine copy drills. Eighteen fields asking for evidence.

SOS SMS has none. No contractors, no jobs, no payouts. Fill both fields with `{TBD}` in every drill. Method and process are legitimate substitutes for the social proof headline: how a job reaches the contractor, when the trip charge is collected, and what accepting commits them to. That signals seriousness without claiming a result.

**A second risk specific to this side: earnings.** A contractor copy drill invites "make $X more a month" or "fill your slow nights". Both are income claims with no data behind them. Write the mechanics instead.

**A third: cross-side drift.** Check every headline against `GTM-BRIEF.md` Section 6 before closing the phase. "Guaranteed trip charge" is the headline most likely to be undercut by a customer-side promise.

This is where a fabricated testimonial enters a run that was otherwise honest, because the template asks nine times and templates get filled.

## Phase 7. Customer Journey Funnel

**Runs as written,** with one exception. Stage 7, "Renewal and Loyalty", assumes a recurring contract. Here it reads as staying active, still accepting jobs after the first month, and referring another contractor.

**Value realization** is the first completed job with the trip charge paid out. Everything before it is onboarding.

**Add these to Primary Journey Blockers:**

- **Onboarding friction.** License and insurance verification, and payout setup with whichever payment provider holds the trip charge, which usually means identity and bank checks. These are the first places a free signup stalls.
- **Can SOS SMS actually pay the contractor.** Payout timing is candidate item 8. A contractor who waits weeks for a trip charge will not treat it as guaranteed.
- **First-job silence.** A contractor who joins and sees no request in the first weeks leaves before value realization. This is a density problem showing up as a churn problem, and it is why Run 2 waits on Step 5.2.

## Phase 8. Assets and Collaterals

**Assumes three campaigns, hardcoded:** LinkedIn Outreach via HeyReach, Cold Email via Instantly, and LinkedIn Ads.

**Why it breaks:** SOS SMS is product-led with no sales team (brief, Classification). Nobody calls or visits contractors; contractors sign themselves up by text. Trade owner-operators are also not a LinkedIn audience the way software buyers are (working assumption).

**Instead,** keep the three-channel by three-funnel-stage grid with these three, decided by the founders on 2026-10-01:

1. **Cold email.** The skill's own default, kept: sequences to licensed contractors in the launch zone, with the Lead Cost Check as the hook and self-serve sign-up by text as the only call to action. Commercial email rules apply to business email too: accurate headers, honest subject lines, a postal address, and an opt-out honored within 10 business days (S73).
2. **Partnership program.** Supply houses, trade associations, trade schools, and tools contractors already use. Partners place SOS SMS in front of contractors; contractors sign up on their own.
3. **In-product referral loop.** Not a sales channel: after a contractor's fifth paid trip, the product asks which other trade they trust and gives them a link to send. Keep or drop it; it costs nothing to run.

**Texting rule.** Marketing texts need prior written consent (S31). A contractor gets texts only after starting sign-up themselves (for example by texting JOIN).

## Phase 9. Pricing and Packaging

**Assumes a subscription.** Value metrics per user, seat, usage, outcome, or capability, and four plans arranged as Decoy, Hero, and Anchor.

**Why it breaks:** contractors pay nothing to join, by design. Four plans of a free product describe nothing.

**Instead, build the supply side of the take rate:**

1. What a contractor pays SOS SMS per completed job, if anything. `{TBD}`, open decision 2.
2. The subsidy question: which side carries SOS SMS's revenue, which side carries the cost of the 90-minute refund (open decision 3), and why.
3. The trip charge mechanics: the amount, who sets it, and when it is released to the contractor. All `{TBD}`.

If a paid priority tier comes up, flag it and do not design it in this run. Paying for priority conflicts with "every matched contractor sees the request at once" and with "accept or decline freely".

**Step 9.3, ROI anchoring, still runs and is the strongest artifact here.** Anchor 2, the alternative comparison, is what a contractor pays today to win one emergency job through a slot 4 alternative: cost per lead divided by the share of leads that become jobs. Both numbers come from Phase 2, dated and sourced, and stay `{TBD}` until then.
